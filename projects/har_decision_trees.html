Human Activity Recognition with Decision Trees

Building a decision tree from scratch, then using decision trees to recognise six everyday activities from smartphone accelerometer data.

Harshada Kale·Machine Learning course assignment

Overview

This project has two parts. In the first, I implemented a decision tree from scratch in Python and tested it on classification and regression problems. In the second, I used decision trees to solve a real problem: Human Activity Recognition (HAR), where a model decides what a person is doing from the signals of an accelerometer.

Part 1: A decision tree from scratch

The tree handles four cases: discrete or real-valued features, and discrete or real-valued outputs. The model itself only takes real inputs, so discrete attributes are one-hot encoded first.

  • Classification splits are chosen by information gain, using either entropy or the Gini index.
  • Regression splits are chosen by information gain using mean squared error.
  • The tree can be plotted, and the metrics (accuracy, per-class precision and recall) are also written by hand.

I tested it on a synthetic two-feature classification dataset with a 70/30 train–test split, and chose the tree depth with nested 5-fold cross-validation: the inner loop picks the depth, and the outer loop estimates how well that choice generalises. The regression case was tried on the UCI auto-mpg (fuel efficiency) data and compared with scikit-learn's tree. Finally, I timed training and prediction while varying the number of samples and binary features, and compared the curves with the theoretical complexity of building and querying a tree.

Part 2: Recognising human activity

The dataset

The data is the public UCI-HAR dataset: accelerometer recordings of volunteers performing six activities with a smartphone on the body. Three are static (laying, sitting, standing) and three are dynamic (walking, walking upstairs, walking downstairs). I used the raw inertial signals, sampled at 50 Hz, and kept the first 10 seconds of every recording, which is 500 samples per axis. The samples were then split into training, validation and test sets.

Looking at the signals

Before training anything I plotted one waveform for each activity. The three walking activities show clear periodic motion, while laying, sitting and standing are almost flat and differ mainly in the gravity component on each axis, which is why they are hard to tell apart by eye.

To check whether the static and dynamic groups even need a learned model, I looked at the total acceleration, accx² + accy² + accz². This quantity varies strongly during movement and stays nearly constant when the person is still. Separating static from dynamic is therefore easy, and the difficult part is telling the activities within each group apart.

Three ways to represent the same data

The main experiment compares three representations of a recording, each fed to the same kind of model:

  • Raw accelerometer data500 samples × 3 axes, used directly
  • TSFEL featuresStatistical, temporal and spectral features computed automatically from the signal
  • Dataset featuresThe hand-engineered features provided with UCI-HAR

For each representation I used PCA to compress it to two dimensions and plotted the activities. PCA on the total acceleration alone mixes the classes heavily, whereas the feature-based projections form far cleaner clusters, so engineered features give the more informative picture of the data.

I also computed the correlation matrix of the TSFEL and dataset features. Many features are strongly correlated with one another, so a large share of them are redundant and could be dropped with little loss of information.

Training decision trees

I trained one scikit-learn decision tree on each representation and evaluated all three with accuracy, precision, recall and a confusion matrix. The confusion matrices are the most useful output here, because they show which activities the model mixes up and not only how often it is wrong.

Model complexity

A deeper tree can fit more detail, but it can also memorise the training data. To see this trade-off I trained trees of depth 2 to 8 on all three representations and plotted test accuracy against depth.

I then looked at where the models do badly. Because the static activities have near-identical motion, sitting and standing are the natural candidates for confusion, and the error analysis follows the confusion matrices above.

Data collection in the wild

A model that works on a clean benchmark is not necessarily useful, so I recorded my own data. Using a phone app (Physics Toolbox Suite), I recorded 3–5 samples of each activity, collecting at least 15 seconds each and trimming the edges to leave 10 seconds (500 samples at 50 Hz). I filmed the recordings as well. To keep the data comparable, the phone stayed in the same position and orientation for every activity, since changing the alignment changes the signal and hurts the model.

I then ran two tests: first, the decision tree trained on UCI-HAR applied directly to my recordings, picking the dataset version closest to how my data was captured; second, my own data with the preprocessing and featurisation I judged appropriate. Comparing the two shows how far a model trained on one set of people and devices carries over to a new phone, a new person and a new way of holding the device.

What I took from it

  • Features matter. The same model behaves very differently on raw signals than on engineered features.
  • Static and dynamic activities are different problems. Motion energy separates them, but the activities inside a group need finer cues.
  • Complexity has a cost. Depth helps until the tree starts fitting noise, which is why the depth is chosen with validation.
  • Real data is messier. Device placement and orientation change the signal, so a benchmark score is only a starting point.
