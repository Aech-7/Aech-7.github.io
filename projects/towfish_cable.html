Towfish Cable Dynamics

Estimating the shape of a towed underwater cable, and so the position of the towfish at its end, with a lumped-mass model.

Harshada Kale·IIT Gandhinagar
  • Python
  • Lumped-mass model
  • Velocity Verlet
  • BDF solver

The problem

A towfish is an underwater vehicle towed behind a ship on a long cable. It carries sonar and other instruments for seafloor mapping, pipeline inspection and oceanographic measurements. All of that data only makes sense if we know exactly where the towfish is, and that is hard to know. Sensors on the towfish are noisy, and the cable can bend and trail in ways that depend on the ship's speed, the cable length, the water current and the cable's drag.

The idea is to combine sensor data with a physical model of the cable, so the towfish position can be estimated from the cable shape. This project builds and compares such models.

A lumped-mass model

There are several ways to model a cable. Finite element methods capture axial stretching and bending stiffness but are heavy. A lumped-mass model is cheap enough for real-time simulation: the cable is divided into N massless segments, and the mass of each is concentrated at the node where segments connect. Hydrodynamic drag, buoyancy and gravity act directly on the nodes. Torsional and bending stiffness and internal damping are ignored to keep the model simple.

I simplified the drag too. Rather than separate tangential and normal drag components, each node gets a single modified drag coefficient, with the force acting directly against the water flow:

Drag coefficient of a nodeki = ½ ρ Cd A |Vi|
BuoyancyFB = ρ V g

For the i-th node, Newton's second law gives one balance per direction:

x-directionTi cos θi − Ti+1 cos θi+1 − ki Vx,i = m ax,i
y-directionmg + Ti+1 sin θi+1 − FB − Ti sin θi − ki Vy,i = m ay,i

Here Ti is the tension in segment i, θi its angle with the horizontal and m the mass of each node. The towfish at the end gets its own balance with its mass M, buoyancy and drag.

Method 1: steady-state solution

The first approach assumes the massless links are rigid, so axial elasticity can be ignored, and that the system is in steady state: the cable has a stable shape and everything moves at the ship's velocity, which means zero acceleration. The towfish end has known boundary conditions, so the tension and angle on the last link can be solved directly. From there the solution works recursively back up the cable, node by node, toward the ship.

  1. 01InitialiseSystem variables
  2. 02Steady stateEverything moves at ship velocity
  3. 03Last linkTension and angle at the towfish
  4. 04RecurseFrom node N−1 to node 1

The recursion starts from guessed node angles, and after each sweep up to the ship the angles are updated and the sweep repeated until they converge.

What it showed

The exact mass per unit length was not given in the reference paper, so I swept it. As the mass per unit length decreases, the computed cable shape moves closer to the reference. I also varied the drag coefficient to see how strongly it controls the trailing shape.

Overall the result follows the reference well along most of the cable, but it does not capture the curve near the towfish end. Three likely reasons: the generalised drag coefficient may not predict the shape near the towfish accurately, the bend could be an effect of the axial stiffness that this model ignores, and, most likely, the towfish was treated as a point mass instead of a rigid body.

A bigger limitation is that this method breaks down under dynamic conditions. When the velocity varies with time, the acceleration terms are complex and errors accumulate through the recursion, so the angles never converge to realistic values. It is efficient for steady state, but dynamics need a different approach.

Method 2: dynamic simulation

The second model solves the time evolution directly. It adds the two effects that Method 1 left out: axial elasticity, where the tension in each link comes from its strain through Hooke's law, and the added mass of the water the cable moves. At every time step the solver computes the net force on every node, including elastic tension, gets each node's acceleration, and advances the state with velocity Verlet integration. The time step comes from the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition, using the wave speed along the cable, to keep the simulation numerically stable.

  • Solve for cable configurationCable parameters as input
  • Wave speed
  • Time stepFrom the CFL condition
  • Initialise nodesVelocity and position
  • Strain → tensionHooke's law
  • Acceleration of each nodeForce equations
  • Update velocityAnd repeat

I then handed the same system of ODEs to an existing, more robust solver, using BDF (backward differentiation formula). BDF is an implicit method: the new state appears on both sides of the equation, so a (usually non-linear) system must be solved at every step. That costs more per step, but BDF handles stiff systems, where fast and slow dynamics coexist, without forcing extremely small time steps.

What it showed

Both solvers give similar results. Verlet is cheaper and BDF is more expensive, but BDF is less sensitive to the segment length, which is the discretisation parameter that most affects the answer.

Conclusion

A steady-state recursive solution gives a quick estimate of the cable shape but cannot handle dynamic conditions. The dynamic models, using Verlet integration and BDF, produce similar results at different computational cost. In both, the answer still depends on the segment length, which suggests that some parameter in the formulation is causing this dependency. Finding out exactly why is the next step, since it will show how to improve the model further.
